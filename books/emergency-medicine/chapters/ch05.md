Not every emergency begins with an injury or a collapse. Many begin with a change that is easy to explain away: a patient who is a little confused, a limb that feels odd, a fever that will not settle, a stomach ache that has lasted too long. The skill of this Part is to take these small changes seriously and to know which of them can wait and which cannot.

The Part follows the problems you are most likely to meet. Entry 5.1 gives a way to approach any patient whose thinking or alertness has changed and starts with the cause that can be checked and reversed in minutes, low blood glucose. Entry 5.2 covers stroke, where time decides how much of the brain can be saved. Entry 5.3 covers seizures, 5.4 covers sepsis, 5.5 covers poisoning and overdose, and 5.6 covers abdominal pain and bleeding from the gut.

These entries rely on Part 1 more than the others do. The primary survey, vital signs and history are how you find these problems, and the same order applies. Your scope of practice and local protocol decide what you may do, and the medicines used in these conditions are given by trained clinicians under medical direction.

## [5.1]{.num} Altered Mental Status and Hypoglycemia

A change in how a person thinks, speaks or responds is one of the most important signs in emergency care, and it has many causes. The aim is not to name the cause at once. It is to check the causes that kill quickly and can be reversed, and to call for help.

[What It Is.]{.label} Altered mental status means that a patient is less alert, less oriented or behaving differently than usual. It runs from mild confusion to unresponsiveness. Entry 1.3 gave you the AVPU scale and the Glasgow Coma Scale to describe it, and the trend over time matters more than a single score. A patient who was alert 10 minutes ago and is drowsy now is worsening, whatever the cause.

[Why It Matters.]{.label} The brain is sensitive to its supply of oxygen and glucose and to what is in the blood. A fall in either, an infection, a poison, a head injury or a stroke can change behavior long before other signs appear. Patients who are confused may be unable to give a history, may refuse help, or may be mistaken for intoxicated. Do not assume that confusion is caused by alcohol or by old age until the reversible causes have been excluded.

[Reversible Causes.]{.label} Work through a short list. Is the airway open and is the patient getting enough oxygen? Is the blood glucose low? Are there signs of stroke, head injury, infection, poisoning, or a seizure just before? Is the patient too hot or too cold? Is the blood pressure low? Each of these is covered in this book: oxygen in Part 2, shock in Entry 3.5, head injury in Entry 4.3, heat and cold in Entry 4.6, and the others in this Part.

[Talking With a Confused Patient.]{.label} Introduce yourself by name, speak slowly and in short sentences, and ask one question at a time. Ask the patient's name, where they are and what day it is, and note the answers. Do not argue with what a confused person says. Ask family, carers or bystanders what the patient is usually like and when the change began, because the answer tells you whether this is new. A medical alert bracelet or card may give the diagnosis and the medicines.

[Alcohol and Other Substances.]{.label} A patient who smells of alcohol can still have low blood glucose, a head injury, a stroke or a serious infection, and drowsiness in an intoxicated patient can hide all of them. Treat altered mental status in a patient who has been drinking as a medical emergency, check the same causes, and keep the patient on their side if drowsy, because vomiting is a risk.

[Low Blood Glucose.]{.label} Hypoglycemia is low blood glucose, and it is the cause that is quickest to check and easiest to treat. For many people with diabetes, a reading below 70 milligrams per deciliter (3.9 millimoles per liter) is low, though the level that counts as low differs from person to person. It most often occurs in people who take insulin or certain other diabetes medicines, and it can follow missed meals, extra exercise or alcohol. Early signs include shakiness, sweating, hunger, headache and anxiety. Later signs include confusion, odd behavior, slurred speech, drowsiness, seizures and loss of consciousness.

::: {.procedure}
[Procedure: Help a Conscious Patient With Low Blood Glucose]{.callout-title}

Purpose: to restore the patient's blood glucose with a fast-acting sugar. Scope: follows your training level and local protocol; the product and the amount are set by your protocol and medical direction.

1. Make the scene safe and carry out the primary survey (Entry 1.3).
2. Check the blood glucose with a meter if your role allows it.
3. Confirm that the patient is awake, can sit up, and can swallow safely.
4. Give a fast-acting sugar that your protocol names, as it directs.
5. Keep the patient seated and calm, and stay with them.
6. Recheck the blood glucose after about 15 minutes, and repeat the treatment if it is still low, as your protocol directs.
7. When the patient has recovered, tell the responders what happened and when, because the cause still needs to be found.
:::

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Give nothing by mouth to a patient who is drowsy, confused to the point of not following commands, has a seizure, or cannot swallow safely. Place a patient who is unconscious but breathing in the recovery position, call for help, and wait. Giving medicines is outside many roles; follow your scope and medical direction.
:::

::: {.stopline}
**Stop and call for help if** the patient cannot swallow, becomes drowsier or has a seizure, does not improve after treatment, or the blood glucose cannot be measured and the patient is worsening.
:::

The steps work in this order because swallowing is the safety check. A patient who is awake and swallowing normally can be helped by mouth, and a patient who is not can choke on a drink. Rechecking the reading tells you whether the treatment worked and avoids both a missed relapse and an unnecessary second dose of sugar.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Check airway, breathing and glucose early, treat a conscious patient who can swallow as protocol directs, give nothing by mouth to a patient who cannot swallow, and call for help for any patient whose cause is not clear.

*See also:* Entry 1.3 (primary survey and glucose check); Entry 6.1 (low glucose in children).

## [5.2]{.num} Stroke

Stroke is damage to part of the brain from a blocked or burst blood vessel. It is a time-critical emergency. The treatments that limit damage work best when they are started early, so recognizing a stroke quickly and calling for help is more valuable than anything else a bystander can do.

[What It Is.]{.label} Most strokes occur when a clot blocks an artery that supplies the brain. The brain tissue beyond the block is starved of oxygen and begins to die. A smaller number occur when a vessel bursts and bleeds into the brain. A transient ischemic attack, sometimes called a mini-stroke, is a brief blockage that clears on its own. It is a warning and needs urgent assessment because a full stroke may follow.

Figure 20 shows the main arteries that supply the brain.

![**Figure 20.** The major arteries that supply the brain and the regions they serve.](images/ch05-fig20.svg)

[Recognition.]{.label} The signs begin suddenly. The face droops on one side. An arm or leg becomes weak or numb, usually on one side. Speech is slurred or confused, or the patient cannot find words or understand. Other signs include sudden loss of vision, double vision, sudden severe headache, dizziness, and loss of balance. A short screening tool checks the face, arms and speech and asks the time. Ask the patient to smile, to raise both arms and hold them, and to repeat a short sentence. If any of these is abnormal, treat the patient as having a stroke and note the time.

Figure 21 shows the signs on a face and on the arms.

![**Figure 21.** Facial droop and arm weakness on one side, the two signs that are easiest to see.](images/ch05-fig21.svg)

[Conditions That Look Like Stroke.]{.label} Low blood glucose, seizures with weakness afterward, migraine and some infections can mimic a stroke. You do not need to decide which is which. Treat sudden one-sided weakness, facial droop or speech trouble as a stroke until a clinician shows otherwise, and check the blood glucose while you wait.

[After a Brief Episode.]{.label} Symptoms that appear and then go away within minutes may be a transient ischemic attack. The patient may feel well and refuse help. Explain that this is a warning and that the risk of a full stroke is highest soon afterward, and ask them to be assessed the same day.

[Why Time Matters.]{.label} The longer the brain goes without blood, the more of it dies, and the available treatments have time limits that are measured in hours. The single most useful fact you can give the receiving team is the time the patient was last known to be well. It is the time before the symptoms began, not the time you found them.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Sudden facial droop, arm or leg weakness, trouble speaking or understanding, sudden loss of vision, sudden severe headache, or loss of balance. Call emergency services at once and note the time the patient was last known to be well.
:::

[First Priorities.]{.label} Call emergency services without delay, and say that you suspect a stroke. Note the time the symptoms began or the patient was last well. Check airway, breathing and circulation and the blood glucose, because low glucose can look like a stroke. Keep the patient lying with the head slightly raised if they are comfortable, or on their side if drowsy. Give nothing by mouth, because swallowing may be impaired. Do not give aspirin or any medicine unless medical direction tells you to, since some strokes are caused by bleeding. Giving medicines is outside many roles; follow your scope and medical direction.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Screen quickly, call early, record the last known well time, check glucose, protect the airway, and give nothing by mouth.

*See also:* Entry 1.3 (primary survey); Entry 5.1 (altered mental status); Entry 6.4 (older adults).

## [5.3]{.num} Seizures

A seizure is a burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. It can look dramatic, and the most useful thing a responder can do is also the simplest: stay calm, protect the patient from injury, and watch the clock.

[What It Is.]{.label} Seizures take many forms. A generalized tonic-clonic seizure causes loss of consciousness, stiffening and rhythmic jerking of the limbs, and is followed by a period of drowsiness and confusion. Other seizures cause staring, brief lapses, or movements of part of the body. Seizures have many causes, including epilepsy, low blood glucose, head injury, fever in young children, alcohol withdrawal, poisoning and stroke.

[First Aid During a Seizure.]{.label} Note the time it begins. Ease the patient to the floor if they are standing, move hard objects away, and cushion the head. Do not hold the patient down, and do not put anything in their mouth; a person cannot swallow their tongue. Loosen tight clothing at the neck. Turn them onto their side as soon as the jerking stops, and keep the airway open.

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Do not restrain the patient. Do not put anything in the mouth. Do not give food or drink until the patient is fully alert. Stay with them until they recover.
:::

[Long and Repeated Seizures.]{.label} A seizure that goes on for 5 minutes or more is a medical emergency, as is a series of seizures with no recovery between them. Treatment is given by clinicians. Your role is to protect the patient, keep the airway open as well as you can, and tell the dispatcher how long the seizure has lasted. Look for a medical alert bracelet or card and ask whether the patient has a seizure plan.

[Fever and Seizures in Children.]{.label} Young children can have a seizure when a fever rises quickly. It is frightening and usually brief, but the first one needs assessment by a clinician, and Entry 6.1 returns to children.

[After the Seizure.]{.label} The patient will be drowsy and confused, and may not remember. Check breathing, speak calmly, and tell them where they are. Look for injuries. Check the blood glucose if your role allows.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

A seizure that lasts 5 minutes or longer, repeated seizures without recovery between them, difficulty breathing afterward, an injury during the seizure, a first seizure, a seizure in water, a seizure in pregnancy, or a patient with diabetes. Call emergency services at once.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Note the time, protect the patient, turn them onto their side afterward, check breathing and glucose, and call for help for any red flag.

*See also:* Entry 2.2 (recovery position); Entry 5.1 (altered mental status); Entry 6.1 (fever seizures in children).

## [5.4]{.num} Sepsis

Sepsis is the body's life-threatening response to an infection. The infection may be in the lungs, the urinary tract, the skin or the abdomen, and the response injures the organs. Sepsis can progress within hours, and early recognition and treatment improve the outcome.

[What It Is.]{.label} Sepsis occurs when the response to an infection becomes dysregulated and begins to cause organ dysfunction. When it causes dangerously low blood pressure that does not recover, it is called septic shock. Anyone can develop sepsis, and risk is higher in older adults, infants, people with weakened immune systems, people with long-term illness, and people with recent surgery or wounds.

[Recognition.]{.label} Suspect sepsis in a patient who has or may have an infection and who also has any of these signs: a high or a low temperature, a fast heart rate, a fast breathing rate, new confusion or drowsiness, a low blood pressure, cool or mottled skin, or a patient who looks very unwell. In older adults and infants, sepsis may appear as confusion or poor feeding with little or no fever. Early warning scores, which combine several vital signs into a single number, are used in many settings to flag deterioration, and your service will teach the one it uses.

![**Figure 22.** A sepsis recognition pathway from a suspected infection to a call for help.](images/ch05-fig22.svg)

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

A suspected infection with new confusion, a fast breathing rate, a fast heart rate, a low blood pressure, cool or mottled skin, or a patient who looks very unwell. Call for help at once and say that you suspect sepsis.
:::

[Who Is Most at Risk.]{.label} Sepsis can follow a lung, urinary, skin or abdominal infection, or a wound. Older adults, infants, pregnant patients, patients with diabetes or a weak immune system, and patients who have recently had surgery are at higher risk. A patient who has had an infection and then becomes more unwell rather than better should be assessed again.

[Handover Words.]{.label} When you call for help, use plain words that give the clinicians the picture: the patient's age, the suspected infection, the vital signs with times, and any new confusion. Saying "I am worried about sepsis" tells the team to treat the patient as time-critical.

[First Priorities.]{.label} Call for help early and say the word sepsis. Check airway, breathing and circulation, and take a full set of vital signs with the times. Give oxygen as your protocol directs. Keep the patient warm. Treatment, including the medicines that treat infection and the fluids that support circulation, is given by trained clinicians and should begin as early as possible, so the main contribution of the first responder is to recognize the problem and bring the team.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Suspect sepsis in any unwell patient with a possible infection, check and record vital signs with times, call for help early, and reassess often.

*See also:* Entry 1.4 (vital signs); Entry 3.5 (shock); Entry 6.1 (children); Entry 6.4 (older adults).

## [5.5]{.num} Poisoning and Overdose

A poison is any substance that harms the body when it is swallowed, breathed in, injected or absorbed through the skin. Poisonings include medicines taken in the wrong amount or by the wrong person, household products, plants, alcohol, drugs of misuse, carbon monoxide and chemicals. Many are accidental, and some are intentional, which Entry 6.5 covers from the mental health side.

[What It Is.]{.label} Overdose is a poisoning by a medicine or a drug. The effects depend on the substance, the amount, the time and the person. Common patterns include drowsiness and slow breathing, agitation and a racing heart, vomiting and abdominal pain, seizures and confusion. Some poisons cause no signs at first and become dangerous hours later, so a patient who looks well may still be in danger.

[Why It Matters.]{.label} The history is the most useful tool. The substance, the amount, the time and the person's weight and age guide the experts who advise on treatment. Poisoning can also look like a stroke, a seizure or a head injury, and the primary survey and the blood glucose check will find the problems that need action right away.

[First Priorities.]{.label} Make the scene safe. The substance may harm you too, as carbon monoxide or a chemical spill can. Remove the patient from exposure if it is safe to do so. Check airway, breathing and circulation. Call emergency services and, in the United States, the national Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222, which is staffed by experts who give advice for any poisoning. Collect the container, the package or a sample of what the patient took, and bring it. Do not make the patient vomit and do not give anything to drink unless the experts direct you.

[Common Poisonings.]{.label} Household products, medicines left within reach of children, plants, alcohol and drugs of misuse account for many poisonings. Carbon monoxide, a gas without smell or color produced by faulty heaters and engines, causes headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion, and several people in the same space may feel unwell at once. If you suspect it, get everyone into fresh air first and call for help; a pulse oximeter can read normally in carbon monoxide poisoning and must not be trusted. Alcohol poisoning causes drowsiness, vomiting and slow breathing, and the patient should be kept on their side.

[Opioid Overdose.]{.label} Opioids are a class of drugs that include some pain medicines and illegal drugs. An overdose causes slow or absent breathing, unresponsiveness and very small pupils. Check for a response and for breathing, call emergency services, and give breaths or compressions if the patient is not breathing normally, as Entries 2.3 and 3.4 describe. Naloxone is a medicine that reverses the effects of an opioid overdose. Where naloxone is available and you are trained and permitted to use it, use it as the device directs, and stay with the patient, because the effects of naloxone can wear off before the opioid does and the patient can stop breathing again. Giving medicines is outside many roles; follow your scope and medical direction.

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Protect yourself from exposure and from needles. Wear gloves. If the scene is unsafe, such as a closed space that may hold poisonous gas, stay out and call for help. Do not give anything by mouth to a patient who is drowsy.
:::

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Drowsiness or slow breathing, a seizure, chest pain, a very fast or slow pulse, severe vomiting, burns around the mouth, or any poisoning in a child or an older adult. Call emergency services at once.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Protect yourself, remove the patient from exposure, call emergency services and the Poison Help line, gather the container, support airway and breathing, and stay with the patient.

*See also:* Entry 1.3 (primary survey); Entry 5.1 (altered mental status); Entry 6.1 (children); Entry 6.5 (self-harm).

## [5.6]{.num} Acute Abdominal Pain and Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Abdominal pain has dozens of causes, and most are mild. A few are emergencies, and they can be hard to tell apart from the others at the bedside. The safest rule is to look for features that make the pain dangerous.

[What It Is.]{.label} Acute abdominal pain is new pain in the belly. Causes include a bowel infection, appendicitis, gallstones, a blocked or perforated bowel, an ulcer, and bleeding from a vessel. Gastrointestinal bleeding is bleeding from the digestive tract. Bleeding from the upper gut, in the stomach or food pipe, can cause vomiting of blood, which may be bright red or look like coffee grounds. Bleeding from the lower gut can cause bright red blood or black, tarry, foul-smelling stools.

[Recognition.]{.label} Ask where the pain began, where it is now, how severe it is, and whether it has changed. Ask about vomiting, bowel changes, fever, recent injury, pregnancy, and medicines. Look at the patient's color and look at the abdomen without pressing hard. A rigid, board-like abdomen, pain that gets worse with any movement, or a patient who lies completely still suggests an irritated lining, which is an emergency. Signs of shock with abdominal pain suggest bleeding.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Severe or worsening pain, a rigid abdomen, vomiting blood, black tarry stools, fainting, a fast pulse with cool pale skin, pain after an injury, or pain in a pregnant patient or an older adult. Call for help at once.
:::

[Appendicitis and Other Surgical Causes.]{.label} Appendicitis often begins as vague pain near the navel that moves to the lower right of the abdomen over hours, with loss of appetite, nausea and low fever. The pain worsens with movement, coughing or a bump in the road. Other emergencies that need surgery include a blocked or perforated bowel, a leaking blood vessel and an inflamed gallbladder. In women of child-bearing age, abdominal pain with a missed period or vaginal bleeding may mean an ectopic pregnancy, which can bleed heavily, and needs immediate help.

[First Priorities.]{.label} Check airway, breathing and circulation. Let the patient find the position of greatest comfort, often lying on the side with the knees drawn up. Give nothing by mouth, because the patient may need surgery or may vomit. Take vital signs with the times, and watch for shock. Record what the patient has vomited or passed and keep a sample if the clinicians ask. Do not give painkillers or medicines for the stomach unless medical direction directs. Giving medicines is outside many roles; follow your scope and medical direction.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Look for the dangerous features, give nothing by mouth, support the position of comfort, watch for shock, and call for help early.

*See also:* Entry 3.5 (shock); Entry 4.1 (abdominal injury); Entry 6.3 (pregnancy); Entry 6.4 (older adults).

## Illustrative Case

::: {.callout .vignette}
[Illustrative Case: Not a Real Patient]{.callout-title}

Grace, a nursing student on a home-care placement, arrived for a visit and found Mr. Alvarez, an older man who lived alone, sitting in his kitchen. His daughter had told the agency that he seemed more confused than usual that morning. He did not know what day it was, and his speech was slow. Grace checked the room for hazards and put on gloves.

She worked through the survey she had practiced. His airway was open and his breathing was a little fast. His skin was cool and clammy. He was alert enough to say his name, but not to answer other questions. His face and arms looked symmetrical and he could squeeze her hands equally, so she did not think he was having a stroke. She asked her supervisor by phone, and was told to check his blood glucose, which was low. The supervisor told her to follow the protocol for a conscious patient who could swallow. Grace helped him take the sugar as directed, stayed with him, and rechecked the reading after about 15 minutes. He was clearer, and his speech improved.

Grace noted the time and the readings and called for help anyway, because she did not know why his glucose had dropped. The paramedics arrived, and she handed over her findings and the times.
:::

## Care Priorities

::: {.callout .priorities}
[Care Priorities]{.callout-title}

In medical emergencies the cause is often unknown at the bedside, so act on the dangers you can rule out quickly and send the rest on. Check the cause that is quick to test and quick to fix, such as the blood glucose, before the causes that are slower to sort. Write down the time that symptoms began or that the patient was last well, because later treatment depends on it. Give nothing by mouth to a patient who may not swallow safely, and call for help for any patient whose cause is unclear or whose condition is changing.
:::

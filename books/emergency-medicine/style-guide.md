# STYLE GUIDE: Emergency Nursing Fundamentals

Applies on top of CLAUDE.md section 6 (writing quality standard). Date 2026-10-29. Awaiting owner approval with the outline.

## Voice and point of view
- Narrator: an experienced clinical educator speaking to a student in the first weeks of clinical placement. Calm, direct, practical. Not a clinician claiming a personal career; the book never says "in my years on the ambulance" or invents any credential (the author field is blank).
- Person: second person ("you") for what a responder does and for procedure steps; "the patient" and "the responder" for description; "we" only for a shared reasoning step ("we start with safety because...") and sparingly.
- Tense: present tense for physiology, assessment and procedures; past tense only inside the Clinical Vignette.
- Spelling and numbers: US English; US conventional units first, SI in parentheses where clinically used (outline section 11); numerals for measurements and counts above nine, words for one to nine except in measurements and steps; decimal point and comma thousands separators.

## Tone (4 words)
Calm. Precise. Practical. Respectful.

## Reading level and vocabulary
- Target: first-year college level (roughly grade 11 to 12). Every clinical term is defined at first use in its home entry, then used consistently as locked in the terminology table.
- Explain mechanism briefly before the action ("blood pressure falls because... so we...").
- Calibrated language ("often", "usually", "in some patients") and honest limits ("recommendations differ between organizations; follow your local protocol").
- Sentence length varies: mostly 12 to 24 words, with short sentences for warnings and key actions. Paragraphs 3 to 6 sentences, one idea each.
- Humor: none in clinical content. A dry, human touch is allowed in the opening overview only, never about patients.

## Structure conventions (5 x 8 in page)
- Headings: Part (Heading 1), Entry (Heading 2), run-in bold labels inside entries (no Heading 3), for example **What It Is.** **Why It Matters.** **Recognition.** **Nursing and Responder Priorities.** **When to Seek Care.** Labels are taken from the profile entry templates and only used when the topic supports them.
- Main explanation is connected prose. Short tables and numbered steps are allowed only for procedures, reference values and comparisons, never as a replacement for explanation.
- **Procedures** use a fixed pattern: a one-sentence purpose, who may perform it ("Scope: follows your training level and local protocol."), a short numbered step list (6 to 10 steps), a boxed **Safety** callout, a **Stop and call for help if** line, and a prose paragraph explaining why the steps work.
- **Callouts** (icon plus label): **Safety** (acting wrongly could harm), **Red Flags** (emergency features), **Scope Note** (training level or protocol limit), **Remember** (one key memory aid, at most one per entry). Callouts hold 20 to 60 words.
- **Clinical Vignette:** about 250 words, past tense, original fictional composite, labelled "Illustrative case: not a real patient", no identifying details, no named institutions.
- **Care Priorities:** closes each Part, about 250 words, ordered priorities in prose (three to five sentences may start with First, Second); it does not restate entries.
- **Review Questions:** 8 per Part (about 700 words with stems), native numbering, one best answer or short case answer; answer key in Appendix A with a one to three sentence explanation.
- Each entry ends with a plain-text `See also:` line. Cross-references are plain text only ("Entry 1.3 set out the primary survey."). No hyperlinks.
- Figure captions: one sentence, plain text, begins "Figure N." and says what to notice. AI-generated images are never called photographs.

## Extra words and patterns to avoid (in addition to CLAUDE.md section 6)
"Rapid-fire", "game changer", "must-know", "crucial" and "vital" as filler (use only for physiology), "life-saving" as marketing, "dive into", "golden hour" (unverified), "always" and "never" except in verified safety rules, "just" (as in "just call"), "simply", "easy", "straightforward" (procedures can be hard), exam-promise language ("will help you pass"), shaming language, jargon abbreviations before their definition, and "ER" in formal text (use emergency department, ED after first definition).
Also avoid: ending every entry on a moral; opening several entries the same way; "not only... but also"; lists of exactly three used as a rhythm tic.

## Safety writing rules (medical profile applies)
- Never tell a reader to start, stop or change a prescription medicine; never give a medicine dose, frequency, duration or route. Verified procedural parameters (compression rate, depth and ratio, ventilation rate, tourniquet and cooling timings, glucose thresholds, reference values) are allowed only with a logged current source and clinician clearance. Drug names are limited to the closed list in outline.md section 1 and always carry: "Giving medicines is outside many roles; follow your scope and medical direction."
- Behavioral emergencies: safe-messaging language for suicide risk; no restraint technique.
- Every dangerous-condition entry has a plain-language **When to Seek Care** or **Red Flags** passage.
- Every procedure carries a Safety callout and the scope and protocol line.
- No self-diagnosis encouragement, no cure claims, no outcome promises, no ranking or exam claims.
- Neutral, person-first language ("a person with diabetes"). Mention sex, age, ancestry or ethnicity only where clinically relevant, and say why.

## Two short samples (fresh, to show the voice; not chapter text)
**Sample A (concept, from the opening of Entry 1.2):**
Triage is not a judgment about who deserves care. It is a way of deciding who cannot wait. A patient who walks in talking, holding a swollen ankle, can sit for a while. A patient who looks calm but is breathing far too fast cannot, and the person at the desk has perhaps thirty seconds to notice. This entry gives you a short, repeatable way to sort patients so that the quiet emergency is not missed behind the loud inconvenience.

**Sample B (procedure lead-in, from a skill entry):**
Before you kneel beside the patient, look at the scene once more. Check for traffic, spilled fluid, loose electrical equipment and anyone who may still be a danger. Your first duty is to arrive able to help, because an injured responder is a second patient and takes a rescuer away from the first. Put on gloves, take a breath, and then begin the steps below in order.

# Glossary {data-label="Reference" .back}

::: {.twocol}

[A]{.letter}

**Abdominal thrusts.** Quick inward and upward presses on the upper abdomen used to dislodge an object from the airway of a conscious adult or child who is choking.

**Acute coronary syndrome (ACS).** A group of conditions in which blood flow to part of the heart muscle falls suddenly; the heart attack is the best known.

**Airway.** The passage that carries air from the nose and mouth to the lungs.

**Altered mental status.** A change in alertness, thinking or behavior compared with the person's usual state.

**Anaphylaxis.** A severe, whole-body allergic reaction that can narrow the airway and lower the blood pressure within minutes.

**Angina.** Chest discomfort that appears when the heart muscle needs more oxygen than a narrowed artery can supply, and that usually eases with rest.

**Asthma.** A long-term lung condition in which the airways narrow and swell in response to triggers, causing wheezing, cough and breathlessness.

**AVPU.** A quick scale of alertness: Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive.

[B]{.letter}

**Bag-valve-mask (BVM).** A face mask, one-way valve and squeezable bag used to move air into the lungs of a patient who is not breathing adequately.

[C]{.letter}

**Capillary refill time.** The time for color to return to a fingertip after it has been pressed; a long time suggests poor blood flow to the skin.

**Cardiac arrest.** The sudden stop of effective heartbeat; the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally.

**Central cyanosis.** A blue or gray tint to the lips and tongue caused by a low level of oxygen; a late sign.

**Compensated shock.** The early stage of shock, in which the body keeps the blood pressure up by speeding the heart and narrowing vessels.

**Concussion.** A brain injury caused by a blow or jolt that can cause confusion, headache or memory loss, with or without loss of consciousness.

**COPD.** Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a long-term lung disease with narrowed airways and damaged lungs.

**CPR.** Cardiopulmonary resuscitation: chest compressions, with or without rescue breaths, to keep blood moving when the heart has stopped.

**Crowning.** The moment during birth when the baby's head is visible at the vaginal opening.

[D]{.letter}

**De-escalation.** Calm words and actions used to reduce a person's agitation and the risk of harm.

**Defibrillation.** A shock delivered to the heart to correct a rhythm that prevents effective pumping.

**Delirium.** A sudden change in thinking and attention caused by illness, medicines or injury; a sign that the patient is unwell.

**Direct pressure.** Firm pressure on a bleeding wound with a clean dressing or cloth; the first step in controlling bleeding.

**Dislocation.** A joint forced out of its normal position.

[E]{.letter}

**Epiglottis.** A flap of tissue that covers the entrance to the larynx when swallowing.

**Epinephrine (adrenaline).** The first-line medicine for anaphylaxis, carried by some patients in a prefilled auto-injector.

**Exposure.** The last step of the primary survey: exposing the body as needed to look for injuries while protecting dignity and warmth.

[F]{.letter}

**First look.** A rapid general look at the patient, lasting about 30 seconds, made before the lettered checks of the primary survey.

**Fracture.** A break in a bone.

**Frostbite.** Freezing of skin and tissue, most often in fingers, toes, ears and nose.

[G]{.letter}

**Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS).** A score from 3 to 15 based on eye opening, verbal response and motor response, used to describe level of consciousness.

[H]{.letter}

**Heat exhaustion.** Illness from heat with heavy sweating, weakness, headache and nausea.

**Heat stroke.** A dangerous rise in body temperature with confusion, collapse or seizure; an emergency.

**Hemorrhage.** Severe bleeding.

**Hypoglycemia.** Low blood glucose; for many people with diabetes, below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L).

**Hypothermia.** A fall in body temperature that begins with shivering and clumsiness and can progress to drowsiness.

**Hypoxia.** A low level of oxygen in the tissues.

[J]{.letter}

**Jaw thrust.** Lifting the jaw forward with the fingers behind its angles to open the airway while moving the neck as little as possible.

[L]{.letter}

**Larynx.** The voice box, at the top of the windpipe.

**Local protocol.** The written procedures of your service, school or hospital that set what you do in a given situation.

[M]{.letter}

**Mechanism of injury.** How an injury happened, which suggests which injuries to look for.

**Medical direction.** Guidance from a physician or other authorized clinician that sets or approves what responders do.

[N]{.letter}

**Naloxone.** A medicine that reverses the effects of an opioid overdose.

**Non-rebreather mask.** An oxygen mask with a reservoir bag that delivers a high concentration of oxygen to a breathing patient.

[O]{.letter}

**Opioid.** A drug class that includes some pain medicines and illegal drugs; overdose slows or stops breathing.

**Oxygen saturation (SpO2).** The percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen, estimated by a pulse oximeter.

[P]{.letter}

**Pharynx.** The throat, behind the nose and mouth.

**Pneumonia.** An infection of the lungs.

**Primary survey.** The fixed sequence of checks (airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure) that finds immediate threats to life.

**Pulse oximeter.** A clip-on device that estimates oxygen saturation and pulse rate.

[R]{.letter}

**Recovery position.** A position on the side that keeps the airway clear in a breathing patient who is unresponsive.

**Respiratory distress.** Difficult or labored breathing.

**Respiratory failure.** The stage at which breathing can no longer meet the body's needs.

**Rule of nines.** A method of estimating the share of the body surface burned, using regions of about 9 percent.

[S]{.letter}

**Scope of practice.** The tasks you are legally authorized and trained to perform.

**Secondary survey.** A slower head-to-toe examination and focused history carried out after immediate threats have been treated.

**Sepsis.** A life-threatening response to infection that causes organ dysfunction.

**Shock.** Failure of the circulation to deliver enough blood and oxygen to the organs.

**Spinal motion restriction.** Keeping the head, neck and trunk in line and limiting movement when a spinal injury is possible.

**Splint.** A support that holds an injured limb still.

**Standard precautions.** The basic infection-control measures used with every patient, including hand hygiene and protective equipment.

**Stridor.** A high-pitched sound on breathing in that suggests a significant partial blockage of the airway.

**Stroke.** Damage to part of the brain from a blocked or burst blood vessel.

**Suction.** Removal of liquid or small particles from the mouth and throat with a device.

[T]{.letter}

**Tourniquet.** A band tightened around a limb to stop blood flow to a severely bleeding wound.

**Trachea.** The windpipe, running from the larynx into the chest.

**Transient ischemic attack.** A brief blockage of blood flow to the brain with stroke-like signs that clear on their own; a warning of stroke.

**Triage.** Sorting patients by how soon they must be seen.

[V]{.letter}

**Vital signs.** Heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure and temperature, usually with oxygen saturation added.

[W]{.letter}

**Wound packing.** Pushing gauze into a deep wound onto the bleeding source and pressing over it.

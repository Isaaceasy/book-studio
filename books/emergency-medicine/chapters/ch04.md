# Trauma, Burns and Environmental Emergencies {data-label="Part 4"}

::: {.callout .objectives}
[In This Part]{.callout-title}

1. Use the mechanism of injury to raise suspicion of serious injury.
2. Describe the order of external bleeding control (pressure, packing, tourniquet) and the steps of each.
3. Recognize head, spine and chest injuries and state first priorities, including the principles of spinal motion restriction.
4. Describe basic splinting and the checks that go with it.
5. Estimate burn size and state first priorities.
6. Recognize heat and cold emergencies.
:::

Injury is the emergency where what you see is often the least of it. A small wound may sit over a large bleed. A person who walks away from a crash may have an unstable spine. A burn that looks minor may cover a large share of the body. This Part teaches you to read the story of the injury before you decide what to do, and then to act on the problems that kill fastest.

The six entries move from the general to the particular. Entry 4.1 sets out how to size up a trauma patient and what the mechanism of injury tells you. Entry 4.2 deals with bleeding, the one trauma problem that can be controlled by a bystander in minutes. Entry 4.3 covers injuries to the head, spine and chest. Entry 4.4 covers broken bones and splinting. Entry 4.5 covers burns, and Entry 4.6 closes with the emergencies caused by heat and cold.

Everything here builds on the safe scene and the primary survey of Part 1. The skills in this Part are basic and non-invasive, and each is taught in a course before it is done on a patient. Your scope of practice and local protocol decide what you may do.

## [4.1]{.num} Trauma Assessment and Mechanism of Injury

A trauma patient may look much better or much worse than they are. The mechanism of injury, meaning how the injury happened, is your best early clue to how worried to be.

[What It Is.]{.label} Trauma assessment applies the primary survey of Entry 1.3 to an injured patient, with two changes. First, severe external bleeding is handled before the airway, because a patient can lose a dangerous amount of blood in minutes. Second, you consider the mechanism of injury from the first look and keep it in mind, so that the examination looks for the injuries that the mechanism makes likely.

[Why It Matters.]{.label} The body hides serious injury. A young adult with strong compensation can keep a normal blood pressure while bleeding inside, as Entry 3.5 explained for shock. A patient with a distracting injury, such as a broken leg, may not mention neck pain. If you wait for the patient to complain of every problem, you will miss some of them. The mechanism tells you where to look before the patient can.

[High-Risk Mechanisms.]{.label} Treat these as a warning that serious injury may be present even when the patient looks well: a fall from a height, a high-speed crash, a patient thrown from a vehicle or a motorcycle, a crash in which another person died, a pedestrian or cyclist struck by a vehicle, a penetrating wound to the head, chest or abdomen, and a crush or blast injury. Age and condition change the risk. Older adults and people on blood-thinning medicines can bleed seriously after an apparently minor fall, and children may look well until they suddenly deteriorate.

::: {.callout .remember}
[Remember]{.callout-title}

Look at the scene as well as the patient. A smashed windshield, a bent steering wheel, a helmet with a crack or a ladder lying beside a person all say more about the injury than the first words the patient speaks.
:::

[Hidden Bleeding.]{.label} Blood can collect where you cannot see it: in the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis and around a broken thigh bone. A patient with a tender, swollen or rigid abdomen, a painful pelvis, or a thigh that is swollen and deformed may be losing a great deal of blood inside the body. Do not press on the pelvis to test it, and do not rock it. Leave the examination of these areas to the clinicians, report what you see, and treat the signs of shock as they appear.

[The Trauma Survey.]{.label} Begin with a safe scene and a look for bleeding. Control severe bleeding at once. Then work through airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, using the jaw thrust rather than the head tilt if a spinal injury is possible, as Entry 2.2 described. Keep the patient warm, because cold worsens bleeding problems. Expose the body enough to look for injuries and cover the patient again. Take vital signs and write down the times. Call for help early, since a patient with a significant mechanism and any sign of instability needs a trauma center, and transport decisions belong to the responding clinicians.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Severe bleeding, a patient who is confused or drowsy after an injury, difficulty breathing, a rigid or swollen abdomen, a deformed or unstable pelvis, a fast weak pulse with pale cool skin, or a high-risk mechanism with any of these. Call for help at once.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Make the scene safe, control severe bleeding first, work through the primary survey with the spine in mind, keep the patient warm, and call for help early for any high-risk mechanism.

*See also:* Entry 1.3 (primary survey); Entry 3.5 (shock); Entry 4.2 (bleeding); Entry 4.3 (spine); Entry 6.1 (children); Entry 6.6 (abuse).

## [4.2]{.num} Bleeding and Hemorrhage Control

Severe bleeding can kill within minutes, and it is the cause of death that a bystander is most able to prevent. The skills in this entry are among the most useful in the book, and they are simple enough to learn in a short course.

[What It Is.]{.label} Hemorrhage is severe bleeding. External hemorrhage is the kind you can see and reach. The three techniques are direct pressure, wound packing and a tourniquet, and they are used in that order of escalation: pressure for most wounds, packing for deep wounds where pressure alone is not enough, and a tourniquet for severe bleeding from an arm or a leg that other measures do not control.

[Why It Matters.]{.label} The adult body holds a limited amount of blood, and a heavy bleed can drain it fast. Bleeding that spurts, pools on the ground, soaks through clothing, or does not stop with firm pressure is life-threatening. The signs of shock from blood loss are those of Entry 3.5. The best moment to act is before they appear.

[Direct Pressure.]{.label} Press firmly on the wound with a clean cloth, gauze or a dressing, using the flat of your hands. Do not lift the dressing to look. If blood soaks through, add more material on top and keep pressing. Hold pressure until help arrives or until the bleeding stops. This is the first step for most bleeding.

[Wound Packing.]{.label} When the wound is deep and the bleeding does not stop under pressure, pack the wound. Push gauze, ideally gauze that contains a clotting agent, directly into the wound and onto the bleeding source, filling the cavity, then press firmly over it for as long as your protocol directs.

[Tourniquets.]{.label} A tourniquet is a band that is tightened around an arm or leg above a wound to stop blood flow. It is for life-threatening bleeding from a limb that has not been controlled by other measures. It works, and it hurts. Apply it high on the limb, between the wound and the heart, tighten it until the bleeding stops, and note the time. Do not loosen it; leave that to clinicians.

[Wounds Where a Tourniquet Does Not Fit.]{.label} A tourniquet works only on a limb. Bleeding from the groin, the armpit, the shoulder, the neck or the base of the skull cannot be stopped with a band, and pressure and packing are the tools there. Press hard and keep pressing, and call for help at once. A nosebleed is usually managed by sitting upright, leaning forward and pinching the soft part of the nose, but a nosebleed after a head injury needs medical help.

[Limb Loss and Amputations.]{.label} A partly or fully severed limb bleeds heavily and is a tourniquet case. Control the bleeding first. Wrap any severed part in clean gauze or cloth, place it in a plastic bag and keep it cool and dry, without placing it directly on ice, and send it with the patient.

[Tourniquet Pitfalls.]{.label} A tourniquet that is too loose can make bleeding worse by blocking the veins and not the arteries, so tighten until the bleeding stops. A tourniquet is painful, and the patient may beg you to remove it; explain why it stays on. If one tourniquet does not stop the bleeding, a second may be placed next to it, closer to the heart, as your training directs. Leave the tourniquet visible so that no one overlooks it.

::: {.procedure}
[Procedure: Control Severe Bleeding]{.callout-title}

Purpose: to stop life-threatening bleeding while help comes. Scope: follows your training level and local protocol.

1. Make the scene safe, put on gloves, and call for help.
2. Find the bleeding and expose the wound.
3. Press firmly on the wound with a clean dressing or cloth, and keep pressing.
4. If the wound is deep and bleeding continues, pack it with gauze and press over the packing.
5. If bleeding from an arm or leg is severe and not controlled, apply a tourniquet high on the limb and tighten it until the bleeding stops.
6. Write down the time the tourniquet was applied and tell the responders.
7. Keep the patient lying down, warm and calm, and watch for signs of shock.
8. Do not remove dressings or loosen the tourniquet; add more material instead.
:::

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Wear gloves and avoid contact with blood. Do not remove an object that is stuck in a wound; pad around it and press around it. A tourniquet must not be placed over a joint. Do not use a tourniquet for bleeding from the neck, chest or abdomen.
:::

::: {.stopline}
**Stop and call for help if** bleeding does not slow, the patient becomes pale, confused or faint, or you cannot reach the wound.
:::

The sequence follows what works. Pressure acts on the vessel directly and is available to everyone. Packing reaches a bleeding source that pressure on the surface cannot. A tourniquet stops flow to the whole limb, which is why it is held back for the bleeding that other methods have failed to control. Writing down the time matters because the receiving team must know how long the limb has been without blood.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Control severe bleeding before the airway, press firmly and keep pressing, pack deep wounds, use a tourniquet for limb bleeding that is not controlled, record the time, and watch for shock.

*See also:* Entry 1.3 (primary survey); Entry 3.5 (shock); Entry 4.1 (trauma); Entry 6.1 (tourniquets in children).

## [4.3]{.num} Head, Spine and Chest Injuries

These injuries threaten the organs that control breathing and thinking, and they often occur together. Your task is to recognize them, to avoid making them worse, and to get help.

[Head Injuries.]{.label} A blow to the head can cause a bruise, a concussion, bleeding inside the skull, or a skull fracture. The patient may have a headache, nausea, confusion, drowsiness, a change in the pupils, or a seizure. A patient who becomes more drowsy, more confused or who vomits repeatedly after a head injury is worsening and needs urgent help. Check the level of consciousness with the scale described in Entry 1.3 and recheck it, since the trend tells you most.

[Spinal Injuries.]{.label} Suspect a spinal injury after a high-risk mechanism, after a head injury, or when the patient has neck or back pain, numbness, weakness or tingling. The aim is to limit movement of the spine until the patient is assessed. Spinal motion restriction means keeping the head, neck and trunk in line and minimizing movement during care and transfer. It does not mean tying a patient rigid to a board in every case, and practice has changed in recent years, so follow your local protocol. In the first moments, a responder can hold the head gently in line with the body and ask the patient not to move.

[Face and Neck Injuries.]{.label} Facial injuries can bleed heavily and bring blood and swelling into the airway. Let the patient sit up and lean forward if there is no spinal concern, so that blood runs out of the mouth, and have suction ready if you are trained. A wound to the front of the neck can bleed heavily and can injure the airway. Apply firm pressure with the heel of your hand and call for help.

[Concussion.]{.label} A concussion is a brain injury from a blow or a jolt. The patient may be dazed, forget what happened, have a headache, feel dizzy or nauseous, or be sensitive to light and noise. A concussion can be present with no loss of consciousness. Any patient with a suspected concussion should be assessed by a clinician, should not return to sport or work that day, and should be watched for the warning signs in the Red Flags box.

[Chest Injuries.]{.label} Chest injuries include broken ribs, a bruised lung, a collapsed lung and bleeding into the chest. Recognize them by pain on breathing, fast shallow breathing, difficulty breathing, bruising, an abnormal movement of the chest wall, or the sound of air entering a wound. They need urgent help. This book teaches recognition only. Give oxygen as directed, let the patient find the position of greatest comfort, and cover an open chest wound as your protocol directs.

![**Figure 15.** The chest wall and lungs with the ribs and the common sites of injury.](images/ch04-fig15.svg)

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Worsening drowsiness or confusion, repeated vomiting, a seizure, unequal pupils, clear fluid from the nose or ears, weakness or numbness in the limbs, severe neck pain, difficulty breathing, or a wound that sucks air. Call for help at once.
:::

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Do not move a patient with a possible spinal injury unless the scene is unsafe or your protocol directs. If you must move the patient, keep the head, neck and body in line. Do not remove a helmet unless your training and protocol direct it.
:::

::: {.callout .scope}
[Scope Note]{.callout-title}

Spinal motion restriction techniques are taught and practiced with supervision. A student or lay responder keeps the patient still, holds the head gently in line if trained, and waits for the responders.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Suspect the spine after a high-risk mechanism, limit movement, use the jaw thrust for the airway, watch the level of consciousness, and recognize the chest injuries that need urgent help.

*See also:* Entry 2.2 (jaw thrust); Entry 4.1 (mechanism); Entry 6.1 (children and the spine).

## [4.4]{.num} Fractures, Dislocations and Splinting

A broken bone is painful and often obvious, but the injury that matters most may be to the nerves and blood vessels beside it. Splinting protects them.

[What It Is.]{.label} A fracture is a break in a bone. A dislocation is a joint forced out of its normal position. A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament. The three can look alike. Treat any painful, swollen or deformed limb after an injury as a possible fracture, since you cannot tell by looking.

[Pelvis, Hip and Thigh.]{.label} Fractures of the pelvis and the thigh bone can cause heavy bleeding and are emergencies. A patient with a hip fracture often cannot stand, and the leg may look shortened and turned outward. Do not attempt to splint a pelvis yourself without training; keep the patient flat and still, treat for shock, and call for help.

[Dislocations and Sprains.]{.label} A dislocated joint looks out of place and the patient guards it. Do not try to put it back. Support it in the position of comfort with padding and splint it as found. A sprain is treated the same way until a clinician has examined the limb. For a minor injury, the familiar advice is rest, a cold pack wrapped in cloth, light compression and elevation, but it applies only after you are sure the injury is minor.

[Recognition.]{.label} The signs include pain, swelling, bruising, deformity, an inability to use the limb, and sometimes a grating sensation. An open fracture has a wound with bone exposed or nearby, and it is more urgent. A limb that is pale, cold, blue or without a pulse beyond the injury is an emergency.

::: {.procedure}
[Procedure: Splint a Limb]{.callout-title}

Purpose: to keep a broken limb still and protect it from further injury. Scope: follows your training level and local protocol.

1. Make the scene safe and treat any severe bleeding first.
2. Check the circulation, sensation and movement below the injury and note what you find.
3. Cover any open wound with a clean dressing.
4. Support the limb in the position found, without forcing it straight.
5. Place a splint that holds the joint above and the joint below the injury still.
6. Pad the splint and secure it firmly without cutting off the circulation.
7. Check the circulation, sensation and movement again after splinting, and note the time.
8. Raise the limb if you can, apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth, and keep the patient comfortable.
:::

Figure 16 shows a limb splinted with the joints above and below held still.

![**Figure 16.** A splint that holds the joint above and the joint below a broken limb.](images/ch04-fig16.svg)

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Do not try to push a bone back or straighten a badly deformed limb unless your protocol directs. Do not apply a cold pack directly to the skin. If the limb loses its pulse or becomes pale or blue, loosen the splint and call for help.
:::

::: {.stopline}
**Stop and call for help if** the limb is pale, blue, cold or pulseless, the bone is exposed, or the patient has signs of shock.
:::

The checks before and after are the heart of the skill. A splint that traps an artery can cause more damage than the fracture, and the only way to know that it has not is to compare what you found before and after.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Treat bleeding first, check circulation, sensation and movement before and after, splint the joint above and below, pad and secure, and call for help for any limb that is pale, cold or pulseless.

*See also:* Entry 4.1 (trauma); Entry 4.2 (bleeding); Entry 6.1 (children).

## [4.5]{.num} Burns

A burn is damage to the skin and the tissues beneath from heat, chemicals, electricity or radiation. The size and depth decide how serious it is, and burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals and joints are more serious than their size suggests.

[What It Is.]{.label} Burns are grouped by depth. A superficial burn reddens the skin and is painful. A partial-thickness burn blisters. A full-thickness burn may look white, leathery or charred and may be painless because the nerves are destroyed. A burn can deepen over hours.

[Estimating Size.]{.label} The size is stated as a percentage of the total body surface area (TBSA). In adults, the rule of nines assigns about 9 percent to the head and neck, 9 percent to each arm, 18 percent to each leg, 18 percent to the front of the trunk, 18 percent to the back of the trunk and 1 percent to the genital area. Each of the head, arm and leg shares is split evenly between the front and the back. A quick alternative is that the palm of the patient's hand, with the fingers, is about 1 percent of the body surface.

Figure 17 shows the body divided into areas for the rule of nines.

![**Figure 17.** The adult body in front and back views, with each region's share of the body surface.](images/ch04-fig17.svg)

[Chemical, Electrical and Smoke Injuries.]{.label} Chemical burns continue as long as the chemical is on the skin. Brush off any dry powder first, then flush with large amounts of running water, and take care that the runoff does not reach you or another person. Electrical injuries can leave a small entry burn and serious damage beneath, and they can cause rhythm problems; do not touch the patient until the current is off, then assess and call for help. Smoke inhalation causes burns inside the airway. Hoarseness, soot around the mouth or nose, a cough and singed facial hair all point to it. Treat these as airway emergencies.

[Children and Older Adults.]{.label} Children have thinner skin and a larger share of the body surface in the head, so a burn is deeper and larger for the same exposure. Older adults also have thin skin and often several illnesses. Burns in both groups need earlier help, and a burn in a child with an unclear history can be a sign of abuse, which Entry 6.6 covers.

[First Priorities.]{.label} Stop the burning process and make sure the scene is safe. Remove the patient from the source. Remove rings and tight clothing near the burn before swelling begins, unless stuck to the skin. Cool the burn with cool running water for as long as your protocol directs. Cover it loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing. Keep the patient warm elsewhere, since large burns cause heat loss. Check the airway if the burn is on the face or was caused by smoke or flame in a closed space, because swelling can close it. Do not apply creams, ice or butter, and do not break blisters.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints, burns that go all the way around a limb, electrical or chemical burns, burns with smoke inhalation, hoarseness or soot around the mouth, and burns in children and older adults. Call for help at once.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Stop the burning, cool the burn as directed, cover it loosely, protect the airway, keep the patient warm, and call for help for any significant burn.

*See also:* Entry 2.1 (airway); Entry 6.1 (burns in children).

## [4.6]{.num} Heat and Cold Emergencies

The body works only in a narrow temperature range. Heat or cold can push it past that range, and the most severe forms are emergencies.

[Heat Illness.]{.label} Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea and a fast pulse. Heat stroke is the emergency form. The body temperature rises to a dangerous level, and the patient becomes confused, faints, has a seizure or collapses. Move the patient to a cool place, remove excess clothing, call for help, and cool the body quickly with whatever method your protocol directs, such as cool water or wet cloths with fanning. Give cool drinks only to a patient who is fully alert.

[Cold Illness.]{.label} Hypothermia is a fall in the body temperature. Early signs are shivering, clumsiness and confusion. As it worsens, shivering stops, the patient becomes drowsy and the pulse slows. Move the patient from the cold, handle them gently, replace wet clothing with dry, insulate from the ground and the air, and call for help. Do not rub the limbs or give alcohol.

[Who Is at Risk.]{.label} Infants, older adults, people who work or exercise outdoors, people with long-term illness, and people taking some medicines are at higher risk of heat illness. Cold illness is more likely in people who are wet, tired, hungry, intoxicated, or unable to move, and it can occur at temperatures that are above freezing. Always ask how long the patient has been exposed and what they were wearing.

[Frostbite.]{.label} In frostbite, the tissue itself freezes, most often in the fingers, toes, ears and nose. The skin may look white or waxy and feel numb. Move the patient to shelter, remove wet or tight clothing and rings, and cover the area loosely with dry material. Do not rub the area, and do not rewarm it if there is a chance it could freeze again before the patient reaches care.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Recognize confusion in a hot or cold setting as an emergency, cool or warm the patient as directed, handle gently, and call for help.

## Illustrative Case

::: {.callout .vignette}
[Illustrative Case: Not a Real Patient]{.callout-title}

Tomas, a paramedic student, was cycling on a trail when he came across a rider who had fallen on a rocky section. The rider, Ellie, sat beside her bike, holding her thigh. There was blood running through her fingers onto the ground. Tomas stopped, looked up and down the trail, and put on the gloves from his bag. He called the emergency number and gave the location.

He pressed a folded jacket firmly against the wound with both hands. Blood soaked through, so he added more on top and kept pressing. It did not slow. Ellie went pale and said she felt cold. Tomas reached for the tourniquet in his kit, applied it high on the thigh above the wound, and tightened it until the bleeding stopped. He wrote the time on her arm with a pen. He kept her lying down, covered her with his jacket, and talked to her until the ambulance arrived. He handed over what he had done and the time.
:::

## Care Priorities

::: {.callout .priorities}
[Care Priorities]{.callout-title}

In injury, bleeding that can be stopped is dealt with before anything else, and the spine is protected until it is cleared. When a patient has several injuries, the one that threatens life in minutes comes first, even if a different injury looks worse. Cooling and warming protect the patient from the environment as much as from the injury. If a technique is beyond your training, keep the patient still, press on bleeding, and stay until help takes over.
:::

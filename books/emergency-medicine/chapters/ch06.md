Everything so far has used one patient as the model: an adult of average build, healthy until the emergency. Real patients vary. A child is not a small adult, a pregnant patient has two lives to protect, and an older adult may show a serious illness in an unexpected way. A person in distress of mind needs a different kind of care than a person in distress of body, and any patient may be a victim of harm that nobody has named.

This Part adapts the habits of Parts 1 to 5 to these patients. Entry 6.1 gives a way to assess a child and shows how children differ. Entry 6.2 covers the changes to choking relief, breathing support and CPR for infants and children. Entry 6.3 deals with pregnancy. Entry 6.4 covers older adults, Entry 6.5 covers behavioral and mental health emergencies, and Entry 6.6 covers abuse, neglect and violence.

Each entry adds only what differs. Where the adult method still applies, the text points back to the home entry and does not repeat it. As always, what you may do depends on your scope of practice and local protocol.

## [6.1]{.num} Children: Assessment and How They Differ

Children get sick differently from adults, and they compensate longer and then fail faster. A child who is breathing hard and still playful is in a very different place from a child who is breathing hard and has gone quiet. This entry gives you a structured way to look at a child before you touch them, and then lists the main ways they differ.

[What It Is.]{.label} The pediatric assessment is a rapid, hands-off look at three things: the child's appearance, the work of breathing, and the circulation to the skin. It takes a few seconds, and it works from the doorway. Because it needs no equipment, it can be done with a frightened child still in a parent's arms. After it, you carry out the primary survey of Entry 1.3 in the same order as for an adult.

Figure 23 shows the three parts of the assessment.

![**Figure 23.** The three parts of the pediatric assessment: appearance, work of breathing and circulation to the skin.](images/ch06-fig23.svg)

[Appearance.]{.label} Appearance reflects how well the brain is working. Notice whether the child is alert, consolable and interested, whether they make eye contact, whether the cry is strong, and whether they move normally. A child who is limp, glassy-eyed, hard to wake or does not react to a parent is seriously unwell.

[Work of Breathing.]{.label} Watch the child breathe. Look for fast or slow breathing, noisy breathing, flaring of the nostrils, grunting at the end of each breath, pulling in of the skin between the ribs and above the collarbone, and a child who sits in a tripod position, leaning forward on their hands.

[Circulation to the Skin.]{.label} Look at the color of the skin and lips. Pale, mottled or blue skin suggests poor circulation or poor oxygen supply. Compare it with the child's normal color.

[Talking With Children and Parents.]{.label} Get down to the child's level, use a calm voice, and explain what you are doing in simple words. Let a small child stay in a parent's arms for as long as it is safe to do so, since a frightened child breathes harder and is harder to assess. Ask the parent what is usual for the child, how long the illness has lasted, how much the child has drunk, how many wet diapers there have been, and what medicines and allergies the child has. Parents often notice change first, and a parent who says the child is not acting like themselves should be taken seriously.

[Dehydration.]{.label} Children can lose fluid quickly through vomiting, diarrhea and fever. Signs include a dry mouth, no tears when crying, few wet diapers, sunken eyes, a sunken soft spot on the top of an infant's head, drowsiness and fast breathing. A child who is drowsy, cool and mottled with fast breathing is in shock and needs urgent help.

[How Children Differ.]{.label} Children differ from adults in several ways that matter in an emergency. Their airways are smaller and narrower, so a little swelling or mucus blocks a larger share of the passage. Their tongues are larger in proportion to their mouths. Their heads are large for their bodies, so a child who lies flat may have the neck bent forward, and a small pad under the shoulders may be needed to keep the airway in line. Entry 2.2 described the positions for adults. Their breathing rate is higher, and their heart rate is higher, and both vary by age.

Children compensate for illness and injury by speeding the heart and tightening the vessels, and may keep a normal blood pressure until they are close to collapse. A fast heart rate and a slow capillary refill are earlier signs than a low pressure. A child who stops struggling to breathe, becomes sleepy and has a falling heart rate is in danger, and this is a late and serious sign. Fever is common in children and is not itself a danger sign, but a fever with a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade under pressure, lethargy or poor feeding needs urgent care.

Entry 2.4 described asthma, and it applies to children with the same signs. Entry 4.2 described tourniquets, and the same limits apply. Low blood glucose is more common in small children who are ill, and a glucose check belongs in the assessment of any drowsy child. Entry 5.1 described the causes. Entry 5.3 covered seizures, and a first seizure with fever in a young child needs assessment. Entry 6.6 covers signs of abuse, and any injury that does not fit the story needs attention.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

A child who is limp, hard to wake or inconsolable, grunting or struggling to breathe, blue or gray, with a rash that does not fade, a stiff neck, repeated vomiting, or a fever in a very young infant. Call for help at once.
:::

[Scope Note]{.callout-title}

Children's equipment and medicine amounts are sized by weight and age and are outside the scope of this book. Use the sizing aids and protocols of your service, and call for help early for any seriously unwell child.

## [6.2]{.num} Children: Choking, Breathing Support and Resuscitation Differences

The principles of Parts 2 and 3 still apply, but the techniques change with the size of the child. This entry covers the differences, using the age groups that rescuers use: an infant is under 1 year, and a child is from 1 year to the start of puberty.

[Choking in an Infant.]{.label} A choking infant cannot cry, cough or breathe. Do not use abdominal thrusts on an infant. Support the infant face down along your forearm, with the head lower than the chest and your hand holding the jaw, and give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. If the object has not come out, turn the infant face up, supported along the other forearm with the head lower than the body, and give up to 5 chest thrusts with two fingers on the lower half of the breastbone. Alternate until the object comes out or the infant becomes unresponsive.

[Choking in a Child.]{.label} For a child who is conscious and cannot cough, speak or breathe, the sequence follows the adult method of Entry 2.2, using abdominal thrusts with a force suited to the child's size. If the child becomes unresponsive, lower them to the ground, call for help, and begin CPR.

[Rescue Breathing.]{.label} A child or an infant who is not breathing but has a pulse needs breaths. For a child, give about 1 breath every 2 to 3 seconds; for an infant the rate is similar. Use a mask of the right size and give gentle breaths, just enough to make the chest rise, because children's lungs are small. Your protocol sets the rate you follow.

::: {.procedure}
[Procedure: CPR for an Infant or Child]{.callout-title}

Purpose: to keep blood moving in a child or infant who is unresponsive and not breathing normally. Scope: follows your training level and local protocol; the exact method your course teaches takes precedence.

1. Check that the scene is safe, tap the child or infant and shout; if there is no response, call for help.
2. If you are alone, give 2 minutes of CPR first, then call for help and fetch an AED; if someone else is with you, send them at once.
3. Place the child on a firm surface and check for breathing for no more than 10 seconds.
4. For a child, place the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone; for an infant, place two fingers or two thumbs on the lower half of the breastbone.
5. Press down by about one third of the depth of the chest, at 100 to 120 a minute, and let the chest rise fully.
6. If you are trained, give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions; with two rescuers, give 2 breaths after every 15 compressions.
7. Use an AED as soon as it arrives, with child pads or a child setting if available.
8. Continue until help takes over or the child responds.
:::

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Do not use abdominal thrusts on an infant. Do not press on the belly or the soft part of the neck. Keep the head in line, and do not tilt a child's head back as far as an adult's.
:::

::: {.stopline}
**Stop and call for help if** a child or an infant is unresponsive and not breathing normally, or if you cannot clear an obstruction.
:::

The steps differ from the adult sequence because children most often stop breathing before their hearts stop. Early breaths and oxygen matter more, and an alone rescuer therefore begins with 2 minutes of CPR before leaving to call. Compression depth is stated as a fraction of the chest so that it fits any size of child.

[Preventing Choking.]{.label} Small objects, round foods and balloons are common causes of choking in young children. Responders often meet the parent afterward, and a short practical word about keeping small items out of reach and cutting food to a safe size is part of care. Do not lecture a frightened parent in the first minutes.

[Calling Early in a Child.]{.label} The rule for an alone rescuer to give 2 minutes of CPR before calling does not apply when a phone with a speaker is at hand. Put the phone on speaker, begin compressions and call at the same time. Your course may teach this variation.

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Match the technique to the age, use back blows and chest thrusts in an infant, give gentle breaths, and use the AED with child pads where possible.

*See also:* Entry 2.2 (choking); Entry 3.4 (CPR and AED); Entry 6.1 (child assessment).

## [6.3]{.num} Pregnancy Emergencies and Imminent Birth

A pregnant patient has two lives in the balance, and the best care for the baby is good care of the mother. The principles of Parts 1 to 5 still apply, with a few adaptations.

[Positioning.]{.label} In the later months of pregnancy, the enlarged uterus can press on the large vessel that returns blood to the heart when the patient lies flat on her back, causing faintness and a fall in blood pressure. When a pregnant patient needs to lie down, place her on her left side, or tilt her to the left with a pillow or rolled blanket under the right hip, so that the weight of the uterus moves off the vessel.

Figure 25 shows left-lateral positioning.

![**Figure 25.** A pregnant patient lying on her left side.](images/ch06-fig25.svg)

[Emergencies.]{.label} Call for help for any pregnant patient with vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, a severe headache, changes in vision, swelling of the face and hands, a seizure, reduced movements of the baby, a fall or injury, or loss of fluid. A seizure in late pregnancy can be a sign of a dangerous high blood pressure condition. Heavy bleeding is an emergency, and the signs of shock may appear late.

[Changes of Normal Pregnancy.]{.label} In pregnancy the blood volume rises and the heart rate is higher at rest, so a pregnant patient can lose a good deal of blood before the usual signs of shock appear. The blood pressure often runs a little lower in the middle months. These changes mean that you cannot rely on the vital sign ranges of Entry 1.4. Treat the mother's own signs of distress as serious and tell the clinicians that the patient is pregnant and how far along she is.

[Injury in Pregnancy.]{.label} A fall, a crash or a blow to the abdomen can harm the baby without obvious signs in the mother. Any pregnant patient with an injury needs assessment, even if she feels well. Seat belt use in a crash should be asked about, and the patient should be told not to leave until she has been seen.

[CPR in Pregnancy.]{.label} Entry 3.4 described adult CPR. In advanced pregnancy, the same compressions apply, with the uterus moved gently to the patient's left by hand if protocol directs, and the adaptation is taught in advanced courses.

[Imminent Birth.]{.label} Birth is imminent when the patient feels an urge to push, the baby's head is visible at the vaginal opening (crowning), or contractions are very close together. Call emergency services and do not try to stop the birth or delay it. Make the patient comfortable, wash your hands or put on gloves, and have clean towels and a blanket ready. Let the baby emerge on its own, supporting the head gently as it comes. Do not pull on the baby or the cord. Once the baby is born, dry the baby with a towel, keep the baby warm against the mother's skin under a blanket, and check that the baby is breathing. Do not cut the cord unless you have been trained and protocol directs it, and do not pull on the cord to deliver the placenta.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Heavy bleeding, severe pain, a seizure, a severe headache with vision changes, a baby that is not breathing or is limp after birth, or a cord that comes out before the baby. Call for help at once.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Position on the left side, call for help early, watch for shock, prepare for birth without delaying the call, and keep the baby warm.

*See also:* Entry 3.4 (CPR); Entry 3.5 (shock); Entry 6.2 (infant breathing).

## [6.4]{.num} Older Adults

Older adults are a large share of emergency patients, and the same illness often looks different in them. The core habits do not change. What changes is how much weight you give to the vague complaint, the quiet patient and the small change.

[What Changes.]{.label} The body of an older adult has less reserve, so a patient may be seriously ill with few signs. A heart attack may bring breathlessness, nausea or tiredness with no chest pain. An infection may bring confusion and no fever. A fall may break a hip or bleed inside the skull with little pain at first. Older adults often take several medicines, and some of them blunt the body's response, for example a medicine that slows the heart may hide a fast pulse. Chest pain in older adults and the other presentations of Entry 3.2 deserve the same urgent response.

[Confusion.]{.label} New confusion is not a normal part of ageing. It is called delirium and is a sign of illness: infection, low blood glucose, low oxygen, medicines, pain, dehydration or a head injury. A patient with dementia may be confused all the time, so ask family or the carer what the usual state is and treat a change from that as an emergency.

[Falls.]{.label} A fall is both an injury and a clue. Ask why the patient fell, whether they blacked out, whether they hit their head, and whether they take blood-thinning medicines. Entry 4.1 described the risk of bleeding after a minor fall.

[Medicines.]{.label} Ask for the list of medicines or look for it. Some medicines thin the blood, some lower the blood pressure, some blunt the heart rate, and some cause drowsiness or confusion on their own. A patient who has recently started a new medicine or changed a dose may be reacting to it. Do not stop or change any medicine yourself.

[Skin and Pressure Injuries.]{.label} Older patients who lie for a long time after a fall or who cannot move easily can develop pressure sores, and thin skin tears easily. Look at the heels, hips and lower back when you expose the patient, and tell the clinicians. A patient who has been on the floor for hours can also be cold, dehydrated and weak, even if there is no fracture.

[Wishes About Care.]{.label} Some older patients have written wishes about resuscitation or about how they want to be treated in an emergency. If a patient, a carer or a document shows such a wish, tell the clinicians at once, and follow your protocol. Do not delay lifesaving care while you search for a document unless your protocol directs.

[Heat and Cold.]{.label} Older adults are less able to sense and respond to heat and cold, and Entry 4.6 described the signs. Check the temperature of the home.

[Communication.]{.label} Speak clearly, face the patient, allow extra time, check whether they can hear and see you, and involve them in decisions. Do not assume that a patient who is slow to answer cannot understand.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

New confusion or drowsiness, a fall with a head strike on blood-thinning medicine, breathlessness with no clear cause, a fast or slow pulse with faintness, and any change from the usual state reported by family. Call for help at once.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Take vague symptoms seriously, ask what is usual for the patient, check glucose and oxygen, review medicines, protect from falls, and call for help early.

*See also:* Entry 3.2 (chest pain); Entry 4.6 (heat and cold); Entry 5.1 (altered mental status); Entry 5.4 (sepsis).

## [6.5]{.num} Behavioral and Mental Health Emergencies

A person in a mental health crisis needs what every patient needs: a safe scene, a calm responder and a prompt call for help. The skills here are mostly skills of speaking and listening, and they protect both the patient and you.

[What It Is.]{.label} A behavioral emergency is a situation in which a person's thoughts, feelings or actions put them or others at risk. It may involve severe anxiety, panic, agitation, confusion, hearing or seeing things that are not there, thoughts of suicide, or aggression. Physical illness can cause the same behavior, such as low blood glucose, low oxygen, a head injury, infection or poisoning, so check the reversible causes of Entry 5.1 first. Do not assume that unusual behavior is only a mental health problem.

[Why People Become Agitated.]{.label} Fear, pain, confusion, intoxication, withdrawal, low glucose, low oxygen, a head injury and an acute psychiatric illness can each cause agitation, and a person may have more than one at once. A calm approach helps whatever the cause. Ask about medicines and substances, and about recent injury, as part of the history.

[Panic and Heart Trouble.]{.label} A panic attack can cause chest tightness, a racing heart, breathlessness, tingling and a feeling of dying, and these also occur in heart attacks and asthma. Do not assume that a patient with these symptoms is only anxious. Check the vital signs, take the symptoms seriously, and call for help if there is chest pain, or the symptoms do not settle.

[Safety First.]{.label} Assess the scene before you approach. Look for weapons, for exits, and for anyone else at risk. Keep a clear route out for yourself and the patient, stay at a safe distance, and do not stand between the patient and the door. If you feel threatened, leave and call for help. Do not try to restrain a patient; this book does not teach restraint, and it carries serious risks for both of you.

[De-escalation.]{.label} Speak slowly and quietly, introduce yourself, and say what you want to do. Keep your hands visible and your posture relaxed. Listen more than you talk, and acknowledge the person's feelings without arguing about what they believe. Offer simple choices, such as where to sit. Avoid sudden movements, a crowd of onlookers and loud noise. Give the person time.

[Thoughts of Suicide.]{.label} Ask directly and kindly whether the person is thinking of suicide. Asking does not put the idea into someone's head. If the answer is yes, stay with the person, take the concern seriously, and call for help. Use language that treats suicide as a health matter, such as "died by suicide", and avoid phrases that blame or glamorize. Do not leave the person alone, and remove means of harm from the area if you can do so safely. In the United States, people in crisis can call or text 988 to reach the national crisis line, and 911 is for immediate danger.

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Do not argue with, shame or promise secrecy to a person at risk. Do not leave a person who may harm themselves alone. Leave the scene if it becomes unsafe and call for help.
:::

::: {.stopline}
**Stop and call for help if** the person has a weapon, is threatening to harm themselves or others, or you cannot keep the scene safe.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Check the scene, rule out medical causes, speak calmly, ask directly about suicide, stay with the person, and call for help early.

*See also:* Entry 5.1 (altered mental status); Entry 5.5 (poisoning and overdose); Entry 6.6 (violence).

## [6.6]{.num} Abuse, Neglect and Interpersonal Violence

Abuse and neglect are hidden in plain sight. Responders see injuries and illnesses, and some of them are not accidents. Recognizing the signs and acting properly can protect a child, an older adult or a partner who cannot ask for help.

[What It Is.]{.label} Abuse is harm done to a person by someone with power over them, and it can be physical, sexual, emotional or financial. Neglect is the failure to provide care a person needs. Interpersonal violence includes violence between partners and other family members. These affect people of every age, sex and background.

[Signs.]{.label} Be alert when the injuries do not fit the explanation, when the story changes, when the delay in seeking care is long, when injuries are in different stages of healing, or when a patient seems afraid of the person with them. In children, watch for bruising in an infant who cannot yet move around, bruises in unusual places, burns with clear outlines, a child who is withdrawn or very afraid, and signs of neglect such as poor hygiene or untreated illness. In older adults, watch for unexplained injuries, pressure sores, dehydration and poor nutrition, and a carer who answers every question.

[What to Do.]{.label} Treat the injuries first. Speak to the patient privately if you can and listen without judging. Record what you see and what the patient says in their own words, with the times, and do not interrogate. Do not confront the suspected abuser. Report your concern to your supervisor and through the channels your service or school requires. In many places, health professionals are legally required to report suspected abuse of children and of vulnerable adults, and the rules differ by state and role. A report is a request for protection, and you do not need proof.

::: {.callout .redflags}
[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

An injury that does not fit the story, injuries of different ages, a frightened patient or a controlling companion, delay in seeking care, and signs of neglect. Report your concern and call for help if anyone is in immediate danger.
:::

[Nursing and Responder Priorities.]{.label} Treat the injuries, listen privately, record facts, report as your role requires, and protect the patient's safety.

*See also:* Entry 4.1 (injury patterns); Entry 6.1 (children); Entry 6.4 (older adults).

## Illustrative Case

::: {.callout .vignette}
[Illustrative Case: Not a Real Patient]{.callout-title}

Sam was a nursing student on a placement in an emergency department triage area when a mother carried in her 18-month-old, Noor, who had been hot and fussy for two days and was not feeding. Noor lay against her mother's shoulder. Sam looked from the doorway before going near. Noor's eyes opened when her mother spoke, but she did not look around, and she let herself be handled without protest. Her breathing was fast, and the skin between her ribs pulled in with each breath.

Sam told the triage nurse what she had seen. The nurse took Noor's temperature and her breathing and heart rates, noted a slow return of color to Noor's fingertip, and moved her to the front of the line. Sam stayed with the mother, kept her calm, and asked when Noor had last drunk and wet a diaper. The mother said it had been many hours.

A doctor saw Noor at once. Sam recorded what the mother said about the timing and what she had observed, and handed her notes to the nurse.
:::

## Care Priorities

::: {.callout .priorities}
[Care Priorities]{.callout-title}

When a patient does not fit the adult model, begin by asking what is normal for them, since the same finding can mean different things at different ages. Look before you touch, because a frightened child, a person in crisis and a patient with dementia all react to how you approach. Take the quiet patient and the vague complaint seriously. In every case, record facts, tell the people who need to know, and stay with the patient until help takes over.
:::

# The Emergency Approach: Safety, Triage and Assessment {data-label="Part 1"}

::: {.callout .objectives}
[In This Part]{.callout-title}

1. Explain why scene safety comes before patient care.
2. Sort patients by how long they can safely wait.
3. Describe a short, repeatable assessment sequence.
:::

Every emergency starts the same way: someone arrives, takes in a scene they did not choose, and has seconds to decide what matters first. This Part gives you a calm order of thinking. It begins with the ground you are standing on, moves to the patient who cannot wait, and ends with an assessment you can repeat under pressure.

## [1.2]{.num} Rapid Triage

Triage is not a judgment about who deserves care. It is a way of deciding who cannot wait. A patient who walks in talking, holding a swollen ankle, can sit for a while. A patient who looks calm but is breathing far too fast cannot, and the person at the desk has perhaps thirty seconds to notice.

[What It Is.]{.label} Triage sorts patients into groups by urgency so that limited people and equipment reach the sickest first. The groups are a tool for the moment, and a patient's group can change as you watch.

[Why It Matters.]{.label} The quiet emergency is easily missed behind the loud inconvenience. A short, repeatable method protects the patient who does not complain.

[Red Flags]{.callout-title}

Placeholder wording: difficulty speaking in full sentences, a gray or blue tint to the lips, confusion that is new, or a patient who suddenly stops responding. Call for help at once.
:::

## [1.3]{.num} Checking the Scene

Before you kneel beside the patient, look at the scene once more. Check for traffic, spilled fluid, loose electrical equipment and anyone who may still be a danger. Your first duty is to arrive able to help, because an injured responder is a second patient and takes a rescuer away from the first.

::: {.procedure}
[Procedure: Make the Scene Safe]{.callout-title}

Purpose: to stand where you can help without becoming a casualty. Scope: follows your training level and local protocol.

1. Stop at the edge of the scene and look in every direction.
2. Put on gloves and any protection your role requires.
3. Name each hazard aloud, even if you are alone.
4. Remove the hazard only if it is safe and within your role; otherwise wait for help.
5. Tell the person you are there to help and ask them not to move.
:::

::: {.callout .safety}
[Safety]{.callout-title}

Never enter a scene you believe is unsafe. A second casualty helps no one. Placeholder wording for design only.
:::

::: {.stopline}
**Stop and call for help if** the hazard is still present or you are unsure it has gone.
:::
